The Cost of Stale Telemetry in Traffic Engineering: Learned Policies, Re-Tuned Heuristics and ECMP

Abstract

Which traffic-engineering controller should an operator run when its telemetry is stale or noisy? We compare ECMP, a reactive heuristic re-tuned for each telemetry condition, and a learned split-ratio policy trained only on small synthetic graphs. All three run under the same impairment model, in a fluid simulator and an emulated SDN, on 24 held-out Topology Zoo graphs and replayed SNDlib traces. The answer depends on the telemetry. With clean or moderately impaired telemetry, the re-tuned heuristic matches or beats the learned policy. With delay, noise and drops together, the learned policy is ahead. On Zoo graphs it gains +0.033 dScore (95% interval [+0.018, +0.049]) and loses on none of the 24 graphs. It is also ahead in all nine real-trace cells. Its mean p99 regret is lower in every profile (0.017 to 0.019 against 0.036 to 0.092), but this tail advantage is not uniform. It has the lower tail on only 9 to 13 of 24 graphs, and the gap comes from a few graphs where the heuristic does badly. In a delay and polling grid, the heuristic's gain falls as delay and polling period grow, while the learned policy's stays flat. The effect is clear on GEANT, and on Abilene it needs 48 evaluation windows per cell to appear. No single delay flips the ranking. Ablations show that fluid-model features matter and message passing does not. A single tuning run can shift a comparison as much as the effects reported. Telemetry faults are modelled, not measured, so this is not a production validation.

1 Introduction

Telemetry quality is a missing dimension in the evaluation of learned traffic engineering. Learned TE policies are typically trained and tested with perfect inputs; operational controllers poll counters at fixed periods and act on stale, noisy and incomplete views. Heuristics tuned to the latest counters can chase stale information, and a learned policy trained on clean inputs may misbehave. Prior work shows that neural routers degrade under realistic delay (Section 2), so we do not claim that observation. We ask a narrower, operational question:

For a given network and telemetry quality, which of ECMP, a tuned reactive heuristic, or a learned policy should an operator run, and what does each cost in tail risk and in controller activity?

We contribute (i) an impairment model shared by simulator and controller and a like-for-like comparison against a heuristic re-tuned for every telemetry profile; (ii) evidence on held-out Topology Zoo graphs and replayed real traces that the ranking depends on telemetry quality, with the conditions under which it does not (clean telemetry, overload, per-network training data); (iii) an ablation and baseline study that identifies what carries the result; (iv) an empirical evaluation of an ECMP-fallback guard and of a rate limiter; and (v) an audit tool that applies these measurements to a user's network. Negative results (LP-teacher distillation, PPO fine-tuning) are reported.

2 Related work

Learned TE. DOTE [1] trains a network on recent demand matrices to output split ratios over candidate paths by directly minimising maximum link utilisation; our training method is of this family, and we add a differentiable fluid model with a packet-loss term and training under impaired observations. FIGRET [2] adds burst robustness to this family; FlexDATE [3] choose critical flows with RL and reroute them by LP, with INT/SNMP telemetry assumed timely; Teal [4] and CFR-RL [5] target scale or rerouting. None of these treats telemetry staleness, noise or missing counters as an experimental variable (full texts read; DOTE tests demand noise up to +-35% only; Teal's delay is solver latency). Staleness. Roracle [6] is the closest in spirit: it shows that LP-optimal routing computed on the current matrix degrades as it ages (about 20% in MLU over a five-step horizon) and that a learned lookahead resists this; the staleness there is routing-computation delay and traffic drift, there is no telemetry-impairment sweep and no tuned reactive baseline, and it evaluates on Abilene, GEANT and Brain among others. LOGGIA [7] models communication and inference delay for neural link-weight routing in a packet-level simulator and reports that neural baselines stop beating static shortest-path routing under delay; it compares with default shortest-path protocols, studies one impairment axis and has no safety mechanism. EnvelopeTC [8] bounds agent actions and disables reroutes after a staleness limit in a hierarchical data-centre setting. RedTE [9] removes centralised control-loop latency by moving decisions to routers. Safe fallback. Training Wheels [10] falls back to a simple policy in unsafe states; our guard applies this idea, and FIGRET's hedging and Safe Load Balancing [11] pursue related goals by different means. Surveys and robust TE. Deep-RL approaches to TE are surveyed in [14]. Robust TE under demand uncertainty predates learning: COYOTE [15] configures traffic splitting for uncertain demands (we have read only its abstract). Those works address demand uncertainty in the planned configuration, not the age and fidelity of the telemetry a reactive controller sees. Novelty. We do not claim to be first to observe that stale inputs hurt (LOGGIA, Roracle, Teal all touch staleness). What we add is an empirical result: in a controlled impairment sweep that holds all other factors fixed and compares against a heuristic re-tuned per condition, telemetry delay and polling period, but not noise or missing counters, decide whether a reactive heuristic or a learned policy is the better choice, on real topologies and traces. In our literature search we found no paper that sweeps polling period, delay, noise, missing counters and install latency together for centralised split-ratio TE. This is a search result and not proof of absence.

Comparison with LOGGIA. We did not run LOGGIA, and a like-for-like comparison is not possible with the present setup. LOGGIA optimises goodput by link-weight routing in a packet-level simulator with central or per-router decisions at sub-second time scales, while we optimise maximum link utilisation by split ratios over fixed candidate paths at the time scale of telemetry polls, in a fluid model. Its code is public (project page cited in [7]), so a fair test would add our re-tuned heuristic and ECMP as baselines inside its framework and run its learned model there. We list this as future work. Our results should therefore be read as a statement about split-ratio TE under impaired telemetry, and not as a ranking against LOGGIA.

3 Setting

Network and traffic. A topology with link capacities and k = 4 candidate paths per demand; the policy outputs split ratios each interval. Traffic is synthetic (gravity base, diurnal cycle, bursts, hotspots, shifts, flash crowds) or a replayed SNDlib trace [13] (Abilene, 5-min, 48,096 matrices; GEANT, 15-min, 11,460; Germany17 and Germany50, 5-min, 288 each; Brain, 1-min, 9,723, restricted to its 600 heaviest demands = 90% of sampled traffic). Each (network, load) pair uses one scale factor so that the median ECMP max-link-utilisation over the trace equals 0.6, 0.8 or 1.0. This is real-trace evaluation under controlled load scaling: temporal and spatial structure are real, absolute load is not. The scale factors are large and differ by network: to reach a median ECMP max-utilisation of 0.8 the Abilene and Brain traces are multiplied by about 9 and 23, GEANT by about 4, while the Germany17 and Germany50 traces are multiplied by about 0.02 (their native demands exceed the published module capacities by a factor of about 30-40, so capacity units in those files are not meaningful on their own). Some traces contain extreme intervals: the largest GEANT interval is about 12,000 times its median, so after scaling it has a max-utilisation near 10^4; Abilene's maximum is about 17 times its median. Such intervals dominate pooled tail statistics and are the likely source of the 'overloaded windows' of Section 6.4.

Telemetry impairment. The controller observes utilisation and demands through a channel with polling period P (intervals), delay d, multiplicative noise sigma and per-counter drop probability p; installing rules takes L intervals. Profiles: ideal (1,0,0,0), rich (1,1,0.03,0), SNMP-like (5,1,0.05,0.02), degraded (5,3,0.2,0.1). The same channel is applied to real OpenFlow counters in the emulated controller.

Metrics. We report separately: gain (reduction of mean max-link-utilisation vs ECMP), fluid packet loss, tail regret (p99 of per-interval max-util minus ECMP's; positive = worse than ECMP) and flow-update rate. The composite score (max-util ratio + 5 x loss, paired difference to ECMP, "dScore") is a convenience; conclusions do not depend on the weight (Section 6.1). Unit of analysis: for Zoo results, the graph (paired across methods; seeds and training seeds are averaged within a graph and reported as spread); for real traces, the (network, load) cell with independent trace windows inside it; windows within one day-long trace overlap and are not treated as independent.

4 Methods

Baselines. ECMP. Tuned heuristic: EWMA-smoothed bottleneck soft-min over candidate paths with hysteresis, damped updates and age-based shrinkage of stale readings; five parameters tuned per telemetry profile by a 40-trial random search on four synthetic graphs disjoint from training and test graphs (it therefore knows the telemetry profile, a favourable baseline). Static LP: LP-optimal splits for the mean traffic matrix of the training part of a trace, no telemetry. DOTE-style: an MLP on the last three impaired demand estimates, trained per network on the first 70% of its trace (ideal-telemetry and degraded-telemetry variants) with our differentiable loss; evaluated on the last 30%. LP oracle: perfect information, graphs with n <= 25 only; a reference bound, not a deployable method.

Learned policy. A graph network on the link graph with path pooling (Section 6.3 shows the message passing is not what matters; a per-link MLP with the same inputs does as well or better). Inputs: impaired utilisation and demand history (H = 3), telemetry age, and model-based fluid features (ECMP-predicted utilisation, utilisation predicted for the current configuration, per-path what-if bottleneck). Training is direct optimisation through a differentiable fluid model: the policy sees the impaired observation, the loss uses the true next demand, smoothmax(util)/ecmp_mu + 5 loss + 0.1 churn. Training graphs are 40 synthetic BA/ER/geometric graphs with 10-20 nodes; no Zoo or SNDlib graph is used. Variants: randomised-telemetry training ("ours") and ideal-only training; four seeds each. Fine-tuning: the pre-trained model is trained for 6 iterations (~3-5 minutes on CPU) on rollouts over the first 70% of a network's trace with randomised telemetry and evaluated on the last 30%.

Guard. L1: stale or missing telemetry, malformed action, deadline. L2: fluid-model check of the proposal against ECMP on the estimated state, rejecting it if predicted max-util exceeds ECMP's by more than a margin eps. L3: runtime monitor with backoff. The headline guard is L2 only with eps = -0.06, chosen on validation graphs. Property claimed: none formal. The guard is an empirically evaluated runtime filter; we report its fallback rate, false-rejection rate, the share of harmful proposals caught and the regret before and after (Section 6.5).

Rate limiter. A demand's installed split is kept unless the proposal moves it by more than a threshold (half the L1 distance); the number of updates per interval is the flow/group modifications a controller would send.

Audit tool. stale-but-safe audit takes a topology, optional traffic and telemetry parameters and reports best-case headroom (LP oracle where n <= 25), gain with a 95% bootstrap interval over trace windows and tail regret for each method under the user's telemetry, a polling sweep, and one of three outcomes: stay on ECMP, use a named method, or insufficient evidence (too few windows, interval straddling the 5% threshold, or gain concentrated in one window). "ECMP is enough" is declared only when the upper end of the 95% interval of the best method's gain under perfect telemetry is below a prespecified 5% max-utilisation tolerance, never from a non-significant difference alone; the 5% value is a practical choice that operators can change, not a derived constant. It flags telemetry values outside the evaluated range. Its output is an offline estimate, not a deployment recommendation.

5 Protocol

Topology Zoo [12]: 263 graphs split into train/validation/test; we report the 24-graph test_subset, 5 evaluation seeds per graph, horizon 100, load 0.8; paired bootstrap 95% CI, IQM and Holm-corrected Wilcoxon over graphs. Real traces: four networks with headroom plus Brain, loads {0.6, 0.8, 1.0}, four profiles; two independent evaluation runs (4 windows, then 10 fresh windows per cell, 12 for Brain at load 0.8). Tuning and model selection never use test graphs or windows. Interval methodology: Zoo intervals are paired bootstrap over graphs on seed-averaged scores, so they capture graph variability but not training-seed variability (reported separately as seed SD, 0.001-0.002; the GNN-vs-MLP and fluid-feature comparisons use four matched training seeds each, averaged per graph before resampling). Real-trace intervals are paired bootstrap over evaluation windows for one trained model and one trace, so they do not generalise to other days or networks. The 5% ECMP tolerance is applied identically everywhere: "ECMP is enough" requires the interval's upper end to be below 5%; a lower end below 5% gives "insufficient evidence". Evaluation uses 5 seeds per Zoo graph and 12 to 16 random windows per real-trace cell, fewer than the 20 seeds we planned. We train four policy seeds, not ten, and some ablations use one seed. Several baselines and analyses were added after the first results (the re-tuned heuristic, the delay grid and the window-level intervals), and a list of deviations from the original protocol is in the supplementary material.

6 Results

6.1 Held-out Topology Zoo graphs (24 graphs)

The heuristic is tuned separately for each telemetry profile. For every profile we run five independent tuner seeds with 80 trials each on the same four synthetic tuning graphs, and evaluate each resulting parameter set on the 24 held-out graphs with the same evaluation seeds as the learned policy. The learned policy is the mean of four training seeds. We report the heuristic averaged over the five tuner seeds, because the best of five is an optimistic estimate; the spread over tuner seeds is given as a range. A "tie" on a graph means the two dScores differ by less than 0.002, about 3% of the learned policy's mean dScore. This threshold is a practical choice, not a derived value. All intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over graphs (4000 resamples, fixed seed), so they measure graph-to-graph variability and not tuner-seed variability.

Table 1 and Fig. 1 give three results.

Average performance depends on telemetry quality. With ideal or rich telemetry the re-tuned heuristic is better on average, and the difference excludes zero for rich telemetry. With SNMP-like telemetry the two are tied (7 wins, 10 ties, 7 losses). Under degraded telemetry the learned policy is ahead on average and wins 15 graphs, ties 9 and loses none. Seed-to-seed variation of the learned policy is small (standard deviation 0.001 to 0.002).

Tail risk is lower for the learned policy on average, but not on every graph. Its mean p99 regret is lower in every profile, and the interval of the difference excludes zero for rich, SNMP and degraded telemetry (for ideal telemetry the lower end is 0.000). However, the learned policy has the lower p99 on only 9 to 13 of the 24 graphs. The average gap comes from a minority of graphs on which the heuristic has a large tail. We therefore describe the advantage as lower on average, not as a uniform improvement.

Tuning variation is large. Under SNMP telemetry the heuristic's dScore ranges from -0.072 to -0.053 across tuner seeds, which is as large as the differences in Table 1. A comparison based on one tuning run can therefore change sign.

The ranking does not depend on the loss weight in the score. At weight 0 (pure maximum utilisation) the heuristic still wins at ideal telemetry (dScore -0.061 against -0.043) and the learned policy wins at degraded telemetry (-0.040 against -0.030). At weight 20 the learned policy wins on 21 of 24 graphs under degraded telemetry, and the heuristic stays ahead under ideal, rich and SNMP telemetry. The heuristic is tuned with weight 5 in all cases.

On GEANT the DOTE-style model reaches about 18% in every profile, the zero-shot policy 15-18%, the fine-tuned policy 16-19% and static LP -38.6% (traffic is not stationary). Hence: (a) with per-network history a DOTE-style model beats the zero-shot GNN, and fine-tuning recovers most of the difference on Abilene but little on GEANT; (b) robustness to telemetry is a property of any policy that does not rely on fresh counters, not specific to our GNN; (c) what the zero-shot policy offers is no need for per-network training data. Ablations (24 Zoo graphs; one seed for the history and age ablations, and four training seeds each for the full model, the per-link MLP and the no-fluid-feature model): removing history costs +0.003 at ideal telemetry only; removing the age input is ~0. Matched-seed GNN vs MLP: the per-link MLP (no message passing) has mean dScore -0.068 (ideal) / -0.063 (degraded) against -0.065 / -0.061 for the full graph model; seed-averaged paired difference -0.0031 [-0.0054, -0.0011] and -0.0020 [-0.0040, -0.0002], the MLP better on 14 of 24 graphs in both profiles. Its p99 regret is slightly worse under degraded telemetry (0.022 vs 0.019). So message passing does not help; the effect sizes are small and we claim only "no benefit", not that MLPs are better. Fluid-model features: removing them costs +0.0082 [+0.0048, +0.0121] (ideal) and +0.0144 [+0.0085, +0.0206] (degraded) and worsens p99 regret (0.018 to 0.029 degraded); the full model is better on 21-22 of 24 graphs. The model-based features, not the graph architecture, carry the result. Training with randomised telemetry versus ideal-only: the mean differences are within seed noise (~0.002), but the randomised model has a lower p99 regret under degraded telemetry (0.019 vs 0.027 over four seeds).

Real-trace windows in which ECMP's own mean max-utilisation exceeds 2 are traffic surges far above the requested load, and they lie outside the training range (loads 0.5 to 1.1). We compared the methods on 30 Abilene windows at load 0.8 (Table 8). Of these, 27 are typical and 3 are overloaded.

6.5 Guard

scenario fallback false rejection harm caught gain kept p99 regret raw -> guarded

On real Abilene and GEANT traces (load 0.8, three typical windows each) the guard fires in only 0-2% of intervals because the learned policy's proposals are essentially never harmful there (0.0% of accepted intervals harmful; raw p99 regret already <= 0); its value on real traces appears in overloaded windows (Section 6.4). On the 24 test graphs the per-graph p99 regret falls from 0.019 to 0.008 at degraded telemetry (average of per-graph p99), while the pooled p99 above falls only from +0.065 to +0.057: the guard cuts the typical tail but misses harmful proposals concentrated in a few graphs. The guard is most effective under link failures. It falls back often (71% to 80%) yet keeps 74% to 91% of the gain because fallback occurs mostly in low-gain intervals. On real traces the validation-chosen guard barely affects the learned policy (0.0 to 0.8 points of gain).

6.6 Operational cost and emulation

Without a rate limiter the learned policy updates 91% of demands every interval in simulation (Zoo, ideal telemetry); a 5% threshold cuts this to 8% and a 10% threshold to 2.4% with unchanged gain (3.1%). On the real traces (degraded telemetry) the learned policy updates 98.5% (Abilene) and 100% (GEANT) of demands per interval without a limiter; thresholds of 5%, 10% and 20% cut this to 5.1/1.7/0.9% (Abilene) and 6.3/1.9/0.8% (GEANT) while the gain stays or rises (Abilene 26.4 -> 27.6/32.4/36.8%, GEANT 37.9 -> 38.2/38.5/37.7%; thresholds of 0.5 lose gain). The threshold also damps oscillation, so the update-rate cost is largely avoidable in simulation.

Emulation: Mininet with Open vSwitch and a Ryu controller reading real OpenFlow counters, 24 flows, four scenarios (three traffic seeds on a 10-node graph, one 12-node random graph), six policy/telemetry runs each plus a run with a 20% update threshold (28 runs). Simulator and emulator agree: across the 24 runs at the default 5% threshold the correlation between emulated and simulated mean max-utilisation is 0.99, the mean absolute difference 0.017 and the bias +0.010; per-run time-series correlation is 0.83-0.98; the policy ranking agrees (Kendall tau 0.47-0.73 per scenario). Under SNMP telemetry the re-tuned heuristic (0.772) is close to the learned policy (0.763), but it reprograms about 140 flows per minute against 270 to 332 for the learned policy, so the learned policy costs about twice the flow updates. Under degraded telemetry the learned policy is ahead (0.772 against 0.806, loss 0.0080 against 0.0095) in all four scenarios, by only 0.001 in one of them. Under SNMP the two are level on average; the learned policy is lower in three scenarios and higher in one. Loop times were measured with the host otherwise idle. Capacities are scaled down and traffic is synthetic, so absolute rates do not transfer.

Decision latency (single CPU thread, p50 and p99 over 100 intervals): the learned policy takes 2.1 / 4.4 ms on a 12-node graph (132 demands), 11.8 / 19.7 ms at 25 nodes (600 demands), 84 / 114 ms at 50 nodes (2,450 demands) and 311 / 412 ms at 80 nodes (6,320 demands), against 0.3 to 23 ms for the tuned heuristic; Brain (161 nodes, 600 demands) takes 31 ms. Cost grows roughly with links x demands, which is fine for minute-scale control intervals but not for sub-second loops on networks above about 50 nodes with all-pairs demands.

6.7 Audit tool

We ran the audit tool on four real networks with degraded telemetry (polling every 5 intervals, delay 3, noise 0.2, loss 0.1; 6 trace windows), using the re-tuned heuristic. For Abilene it recommends the tuned heuristic (30.6%, 95% interval [27.2, 34.1]; best-case headroom 56.5%), which it cannot distinguish from the learned policy or its guarded version on six windows. For GEANT it recommends the learned policy (37.5% [23.1, 51.0]) and warns that the heuristic is worse than ECMP in 7% of intervals and that no method keeps p99 regret under 0.02. For Germany50 it recommends the learned policy (33.9% [30.7, 36.8]), not distinguishable from its guarded version. For Brain it recommends staying on ECMP, flagged provisional because six windows cannot rule out the rare surge window in which the learned policy gained 21%. With the single-run heuristic the Abilene and GEANT recommendations were the opposite. With six windows and close calls, the recommendation depends on the heuristic's tuning, which is a reason to run the tool on more windows. The tool therefore reports intervals and ties, not only a winner, and says when the sample is too small. It remains an offline estimate; its verdicts have not been validated against any operator decision.

7 Negative results

LP-teacher distillation (DAgger) produced a weak student (6.4% gain vs 15.0% for the tuned heuristic on training graphs); PPO fine-tuning did not change it; direct optimisation through the fluid model fixed this. Randomised-telemetry training did not improve mean performance over ideal-only training. A per-link MLP did as well as the GNN, with four matched seeds each (Section 6.3). Static LP routing is excellent on a stationary network (Abilene) and disastrous on a non-stationary one (GEANT).

8 Limitations and status of claims

  • The fluid model has no TCP, queueing or packet dynamics. The guard offers no formal guarantee. It is an empirically evaluated filter, and it misses most harmful proposals under degraded telemetry on Zoo graphs.
  • Real traffic is scaled to a target load by factors from 0.02 to 23 (Section 3). Trace capacities for Germany17 and Germany50 are not meaningful in absolute terms, and some traces contain extreme outlier intervals. The conclusion is insensitive to the scaling rule (Section 6.2b), but the absolute numbers are not.
  • Telemetry faults are modelled, not measured on a deployed network.
  • We train four policy seeds. The history and age ablations and the DOTE-style and fine-tuned models use one seed. Evaluation uses 5 seeds per Zoo graph and 12 to 16 windows per real-trace cell.
  • The heuristic's tuning budget is 80 trials per tuner seed on four synthetic graphs. Tuner-seed spread reaches about 0.02 dScore on Zoo graphs and about 10 points of gain in the delay grid, so single-run comparisons are fragile. We did not test tuning the heuristic on a network's own history, which the fine-tuned learned variants receive.
  • Germany50, Brain and the history-based comparison (Table 12) use a single-run heuristic (Appendix A) and were not repeated with the re-tuned heuristic.
  • The DOTE-style and fine-tuned models need per-network history, and only Abilene and GEANT have enough. Germany17 and Germany50 have one day of data, and their windows overlap.
  • Out-of-range overload hurts the zero-shot policy (Section 6.4).
  • We do not compare with Teal or other published systems. Teal optimises total flow or maximum link utilisation against LP, NCFlow and POP baselines with accurate demand inputs and treats staleness only as solver run time, so it is not a like-for-like baseline for telemetry impairments. Our DOTE-style model is a re-implementation of the idea, not the authors' code.
  • We do not claim a general performance win, being first to show degradation under delay (LOGGIA), or production readiness.

9 Reproducibility and availability

Code, configuration, trained policies and result tables are in the project repository (https://github.com/Starmarine06/stale-but-safe-te). The environment is Python 3.9 or later (tested with 3.13 on Windows 11 and in the Docker images), installed with pip install -e ".[dev,rl]" (torch CPU), and the 39 unit tests run with python -m pytest -q. The SNDlib matrices are public (Abilene, GEANT, Germany17, Germany50 and Brain) and are not redistributed; they are downloaded and prepared with the repository's prepare command. Topology Zoo graphs are not redistributed; a command in the repository downloads them from the public mirror. Each table has one script, and the artefact map in the repository README lists which script and result file produce each table. Seeds, window selection (random 100-interval windows chosen by integer seed), tuner seeds (100 to 104), the model-selection rule and the bootstrap procedures (percentile, 4000 resamples, fixed seed) are in the scripts and in Section 5. The heuristic is selected by its best tuning score on four synthetic graphs, and the learned policy uses a fixed training schedule with no selection on test data. Emulation (Docker, Mininet, Ryu) runs in real time and needs an otherwise idle machine. A clean-environment reproduction check (fresh clone, tests, data download and a table slice) is recorded in the repository.
